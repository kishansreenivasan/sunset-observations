01 · Method

Observation site and method

All sketches were made from the Dartmouth College Private Swim Dock at 43.704534, −72.298737, looking west across the pond. The red house, the white house, the treeline behind them, and the dock in the foreground appear in every drawing. Because those landmarks do not move, any change in the sun's position between sketches reflects the sun and not a change in where I was standing.

I used a compass to establish cardinal directions and marked a compass rose on each sheet. I kept the observation time near 7:50 pm each session so the sun's position would be comparable across dates.

04 · Reflection

How I made my observations

I observed from one fixed spot on the waterfront at 43.704534, −72.298737, looking west across the water. I chose it because the western view is open over the pond and because the same landmarks are visible every time: the red house, the white house, the treeline behind them, and the dock in the foreground.

I used a compass to establish cardinal directions and marked a compass rose on each sheet. I kept the observation time close to 7:50 pm each session so the sun's position would be comparable across dates.

Two difficulties came up. Cloud cover cost me two sessions, which is the gap between July 26 and August 2 and August 16 and August 23. The larger problem was the treeline. The trees sit above the true horizon, so the sun disappears several minutes before the sunset. Arriving a few minutes late meant the sun was already behind the trees instead of above them, which made showing up on time matter far more than I expected.

What the sketches demonstrate

Because the foreground is identical in all six drawings, the sketches isolate the sun's motion. The sun moved in two ways. Its setting point drifted along the horizon, from about 303 degrees on July 5 to about 290 degrees on August 16, a shift of roughly 13 degrees toward due west, which is southward from my viewpoint. At my fixed clock time near 7:50 pm it also appeared steadily lower, from about 7 degrees above the horizon on July 5 to at or just below the horizon by mid August. In the sketches this shows up as the sun dropping from among the clouds down into the gap in the treeline. Sunset moved 39 minutes earlier over the term and day length fell by about an hour and twenty minutes.

This is what I expected. We passed the June solstice on June 20, so the sun's declination was already decreasing toward zero at the September equinox, which pulls the setting point back from its northwestern extreme toward due west and shortens the day. The opposite result would have been surprising: if the sun set at the same point every night, that would mean earth's rotational axis is not tilted relative to its orbital plane.

However, the day to day change is small near the solstice and accelerates toward the equinox, so the difference between July 5 and July 12 is hard to see in a hand sketch while the difference between July 26 and August 16 is obvious.

How this connects to the course

Horizon coordinates. Everything I recorded is altitude and azimuth, which are observer-dependent. That is why a single fixed position was necessary because if the observer moved then the numbers change even though the sun has not.

The ecliptic and the 23.5 degree axial tilt. The sun traces the ecliptic rather than the celestial equator, so its declination varies over the year. Declination sets both the setting azimuth and how long the sun stays above the horizon. My observations sample the descending part of that cycle, between the June solstice and the September equinox.

The cause of the seasons. The earlier sunsets and shorter days come from axial tilt, not from changing distance to the sun. Earth reached aphelion in early July, at the start of my observing period, when it was farthest from the sun and the days were longest.

Two superimposed motions. The nightly setting of the sun is the daily rotation of the celestial sphere. The slow drift of the setting point over six weeks is earth's orbital motion.
